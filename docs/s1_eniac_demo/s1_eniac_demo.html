ENIAC, 1945

One ENIAC Digit

ENIAC didn't use binary. It stored each decimal digit in a ring counter: a loop of 10 flip-flops, each built from vacuum tubes, with exactly one switched on at a time. Which one is on is the digit. Neon lamps on the front panel showed which one.

Counting with pulses

Numbers moved around ENIAC as trains of pulses: a 7 was sent as 7 pulses. Each pulse moves the lit lamp one step around the ring. Passing 9 back to 0 sends a carry pulse to the next ring. This is a 3-digit slice of an accumulator (a real one held 10 digits and a sign).

Ten switches, two ways to read them

Both panels have the same 10 switches. ENIAC reads them as one digit, so only one may be on. Binary lets any combination count. Click the lamps to try patterns.

Patterns that mean something: 1,024 of 1,024. The biggest number is 1,023.

The same 10 switches hold one decimal digit for ENIAC, or any number up to 1,023 in binary: three decimal digits and then some. Binary needs only about 3.3 switches per decimal digit's worth of information. ENIAC spent 10.

ENIAC's 10 usable patterns land on squares 1, 2, 4, 8 … 512: the same patterns binary uses for the powers of 2. Everything else is dark to ENIAC: 1,014 patterns that mean nothing.

What happened next

  • ENIAC had 20 accumulators, each holding a signed 10-digit decimal number. That's 100 flip-flops for the digits of one number, where binary would need about 35 switches for the same range, sign included.
  • Numbers were fixed-point. There was no stored decimal point; programmers kept track of where it belonged and scaled their numbers to fit.
  • In 1945, the First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC, written by John von Neumann after working with the ENIAC team, argued for binary, partly for exactly this reason: far fewer tubes to store the same numbers, and simpler circuits to do arithmetic.
